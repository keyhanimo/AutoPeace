## 1. Vision and Purpose

### 1.1 One-Sentence Summary

AutoPeace is a fully open-source web application that uses automated AI research loops to forecast the trajectory of the Iran conflict and search for feasible negotiated deals, showcasing its own improving results publicly so that anyone in the world can see AI-powered tools working on a major global problem.

### 1.2 Why This Exists

There is an active, large-scale armed conflict involving Iran, the United States, Israel, and many regional actors. This conflict imposes enormous economic, humanitarian, and strategic costs on dozens of countries and billions of people. Meanwhile, AI systems — particularly large language models — are increasingly capable of structured reasoning, forecasting, and multi-agent simulation, but are rarely applied to consequential public problems in a transparent, measurable way.

AutoPeace exists to demonstrate that AI-powered automated research can produce progressively better forecasts and negotiated-deal proposals for a real conflict, while being honest about its limitations. It should inspire public confidence that these tools can contribute to solving hard problems, not just generating content.

### 1.3 What AutoPeace Is Not

- It is not a replacement for real diplomacy or domain expertise.
- It does not claim that high-scoring simulated deals will work in reality.
- It does not produce a single "correct" peace plan.
- It is not a news site, opinion platform, or advocacy tool.
- It does not take political sides.

### 1.4 Design Philosophy

- **Radical transparency:** Every forecast, every score, every miss, every methodology change is public and versioned.
- **Measurability over eloquence:** The system optimizes against explicit scoring rules, not subjective impressions of quality.
- **Honesty about limitations:** The site prominently communicates what LLM-based simulations can and cannot do.
- **Progressive improvement:** The core loop is autoresearch — automated experiments that retain what works and discard what doesn't. Visitors see this improvement over time.

## 3. Core Tasks

AutoPeace is organized around two primary research tasks, directly derived from the research problem statement.

### 3.1 Task A — Outcome Forecasting

**Question:** Given all available evidence up to time _t_, what is the most likely near-term and medium-term outcome of the conflict?

**This is a prediction problem.** The system ingests timestamped evidence (news, statements, military events, economic data, diplomatic movements) and produces a calibrated probability distribution over a fixed set of outcome categories.

#### Outcome Schema (Fixed Before Scoring)

The forecast distributes probability across these categories for each time horizon (30-day, 90-day, 180-day, 1-year):

1. Continued conflict / no meaningful diplomacy
2. Informal de-escalation without formal deal
3. Limited ceasefire or tacit restraint arrangement
4. Humanitarian or prisoner-exchange mini-deal
5. Sanctions or shipping-related partial arrangement
6. Regional framework deal without full normalization
7. Broad negotiated settlement
8. Major escalation / regional widening

#### Forecast Outputs

Each forecast cycle produces:

- Probability distribution over the eight outcome categories, per time horizon
- Ranked list of most likely scenarios with narrative rationale
- Explicit uncertainty estimates and confidence intervals
- Evidence decomposition showing which inputs most influenced the forecast
- Comparison against the previous cycle's forecast, with explanation of what changed and why

#### Scoring

Forecasts are scored using:

- **Brier score** — measures accuracy of probabilistic predictions
- **Log score** — penalizes overconfident wrong predictions more heavily
- **Calibration curve** — across many forecasts, do events assigned 30% probability actually happen roughly 30% of the time?
- **Robustness under evidence perturbation** — does the forecast change appropriately when key evidence is added or removed?
- **Consistency** — does the system produce similar forecasts from similar evidence sets?

#### Ground Truth and Backtesting

The primary ground truth is realized events. The system uses rolling backtests:

- For each past crisis window, the system receives only evidence available before a cutoff date.
- It produces a forecast.
- The forecast is scored against what actually happened.

The historical evaluation set should include earlier phases of the current Iran conflict, prior Iran-related negotiation episodes (JCPOA talks, 2020-2021 tensions, tanker crises), and structurally analogous regional crises (North Korea negotiation rounds, Libya 2003, Iraq sanctions regimes).

#### Failure Modes to Detect and Penalize

- Overconfident forecasts (assigning > 80% to any single outcome without strong evidence)
- Inconsistent forecasts across near-identical evidence sets
- Forecasts that ignore recent high-signal evidence
- Forecasts that mirror ideological priors rather than retrieved evidence
- Forecasts that cannot explain their own drivers

### 3.2 Task B — Deal Design

**Question:** Given real constraints, preferences, red lines, and domestic political needs, what negotiated package is most likely to be feasible, stable, and sellable?

**This is a search and optimization problem.** The system generates candidate deals and scores them via multi-agent simulation.

#### Candidate Deal Representation

Each deal is a structured object (not a free-form essay) containing:

- **Military steps:** ceasefire terms, withdrawal timelines, force posture changes
- **Sanctions steps:** which sanctions are lifted, in what sequence, with what triggers
- **Diplomatic/recognition steps:** normalization, embassy openings, multilateral re-engagement
- **Proxy-related commitments:** agreements regarding proxy forces and non-state actors
- **Shipping and chokepoint guarantees:** freedom-of-navigation commitments, strait security
- **Monitoring and verification:** inspection regimes, third-party observers, reporting mechanisms
- **Dispute resolution mechanism:** what happens when terms are violated
- **Economic integration steps:** trade agreements, investment frameworks, reconstruction funds
- **Timeline and contingencies:** phased implementation with off-ramps and escalation ladders
- **Domestic messaging package:** for each major stakeholder, how the deal is framed publicly

#### Multi-Agent Simulation Protocol

Each deal evaluation cycle runs:

1. **Proposal agent** generates a candidate deal structure.
2. **Stakeholder agents** (one per stakeholder) privately evaluate the deal against their modeled preferences and red lines.
3. **Domestic audience agents** (sub-agents per stakeholder) evaluate whether the deal can be sold politically.
4. **Negotiator agents** attempt to revise borderline deals to rescue them.
5. **Red-team spoiler agents** attack the deal (representing hardliners, regional spoilers, exogenous shocks).
6. **Judge agents** score feasibility, coherence, and evidence grounding.
7. **Meta-evaluator** aggregates all sub-scores into a single composite experiment metric.

#### Scoring Dimensions

Each deal receives a composite score across:

| Dimension | What It Measures |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder acceptability | Would each simulated official stakeholder accept this over the status quo? |
| Domestic sellability | Can each party plausibly defend this to its own internal audiences? |
| Coalition stability | Does any major stakeholder prefer to defect, spoil, or renegotiate? |
| Robustness to spoilers | Does the deal survive adversarial stress tests? |
| Implementation plausibility | Is the sequencing realistic, monitorable, and internally coherent? |
| Evidence consistency | Is the proposal grounded in actual evidence, not boilerplate? |
| Outcome proxy score | Under the simulation's world model, does this reduce war risk and improve humanitarian/economic conditions? |

#### Historical Validation

To avoid becoming a simulation toy, Task B is validated on historical negotiations with known outcomes. The test is: given only pre-negotiation evidence, can the system generate a deal structurally similar to what emerged, or a plausible alternative that simulated stakeholders rate as better?

## 6. Autoresearch Loop

### 6.1 Core Pattern

AutoPeace follows the autoresearch pattern: the system autonomously proposes experiments, runs them, scores results, and retains changes that improve metrics. This is the engine of progressive improvement.

The design draws from Karpathy's original autoresearch (greedy hill-climbing with keep/discard), AIDE's tree-search approach (branching exploration with backtracking), and GEPA's reflective prompt evolution (natural-language diagnosis and Pareto-based selection). AutoPeace adapts these ideas to a domain where the "code" being optimized is agent prompts, stakeholder profiles, retrieval strategies, and deal structures rather than a training script.

#### The `program.md` Equivalent

Following Karpathy's core insight — "you are not writing the code, you are programming the program" — AutoPeace should maintain a first-class, editable research direction file (e.g., `research_directions.md`) that defines:

- What kinds of experiments the system should try next
- Which dimensions of the pipeline are currently underperforming and deserve attention
- Constraints the system must respect (e.g., "do not change the scoring function," "do not modify stakeholder red lines without evidence")
- Simplicity preferences (prefer shorter prompts, fewer agent turns, and simpler deal structures unless complexity is justified by score improvement)

This file is the primary lever the human operator uses to steer the autoresearch loop without directly writing prompts or agent code. It should be versioned and publicly visible.

#### Loop Steps

1. **Evidence ingestion:** Fetch and process new evidence from data sources (see Section 8).
2. **Experiment proposal:** The system (or a meta-agent) reads `research_directions.md`, reviews recent experiment results, and proposes a change to try — a new prompt structure, a different agent role, a modified retrieval strategy, a different stakeholder-profile detail.
3. **Experiment execution:** Run the modified pipeline on the current evidence pack and/or a historical backtest window.
4. **Scoring:** Score the results against the fixed metrics (Brier/log score for Task A, composite score for Task B).
5. **Reflective diagnosis:** If the experiment performed poorly, the system produces a structured natural-language diagnosis explaining *why* it failed, not just that it failed. E.g., "Iran's security establishment rejected the deal because missile restrictions lacked a sunset clause" or "Forecast overweighted a single OSINT report that was later contradicted." These diagnoses feed into the next experiment proposal, enabling the system to learn from failures rather than blindly mutating.
6. **Comparison and retention:** Compare against the current best-performing configuration. If the experiment improves the score, adopt the change. If not, discard it — but retain the diagnosis for future reference.
7. **Logging:** Record the full experiment — what was tried, what scored, the diagnosis, whether it was retained — in the public experiment log.
8. **Publishing:** Update the public dashboard with new forecasts, deal proposals, and scores.

#### Tree Search for Deal Design (Inspired by AIDE)

For Task B (deal design), the system should use tree-structured exploration rather than simple linear hill-climbing. This means:

- The system maintains a **solution tree** where each node is a complete deal configuration and each edge is a single modification.
- Instead of only improving the current best deal, the system can **branch** — exploring fundamentally different deal architectures in parallel (e.g., a "nuclear-first" deal, a "Hormuz-first" deal, a "humanitarian-corridor-first" deal).
- When a branch stalls (no improvements found after N attempts), the system can **backtrack** to a promising earlier node and explore a different direction.
- The tree structure is publicly visible — visitors can see the full exploration history, including dead ends, which is itself informative.

For Task A (forecasting), linear hill-climbing is more appropriate since there is a single pipeline being refined, not a space of alternative structures.

#### Pareto Frontier of Deals (Inspired by GEPA)

Rather than maintaining only a single "best deal," the system should maintain a **Pareto frontier** of deals — a set of deals that are each best on at least one scoring dimension. For example:

- Deal A might have the highest stakeholder acceptability but weaker robustness.
- Deal B might be the most robust to spoilers but harder to sell domestically.
- Deal C might have the best implementation plausibility but lower stakeholder acceptability.

All three are valuable and informative. The public dashboard should display the Pareto frontier, not just the single highest-composite-score deal. This gives visitors a richer understanding of the tradeoff space: "Here are the deals that represent different tradeoff choices, and here's what you give up to get each advantage."

The composite-score deal is still highlighted as the "recommended" deal, but the frontier shows alternatives that might be preferable depending on which dimension a real negotiator values most.

#### Cross-Model Evaluation

To ensure evaluation integrity, AutoPeace uses models from different providers for different roles in the pipeline. The system has API access to OpenAI, Anthropic (Claude), and Google Gemini models. The key principle: the model that generates a deal should not be the same model that evaluates it. For example, if Claude generates a deal proposal, GPT or Gemini stakeholder agents evaluate it — and vice versa. This cross-pollination naturally prevents the kind of self-reinforcing patterns that single-model loops can develop.

#### What Can Be Varied Between Experiments

- Prompts and system instructions
- Agent roles and persona definitions
- Memory and context architectures
- Evidence retrieval and ranking methods
- Decomposition strategies (how tasks are broken into subtasks)
- Deliberation length and chain-of-thought depth
- Debate vs. self-consistency vs. ensemble methods
- Stakeholder-profile richness and specificity
- Domestic-audience modeling depth
- Scoring weight distributions
- Red-team intensity and spoiler scenarios
- Deal architecture (which term categories are included, how they are sequenced)
- Number and structure of negotiation rounds in the multi-agent loop

### 6.2 Run Cadence

- **Default:** Daily (overnight run, results published each morning).
- **Configurable:** An admin setting controls the cadence. Can be adjusted to more or less frequent based on token usage costs, API rate limits, computational budget, or editorial considerations.
- **Admin controls:**
  - Frequency: hourly / daily / every N days / weekly / manual trigger
  - Budget cap: maximum token spend per cycle
  - Pause/resume toggle
  - Force-run button for manual triggering after major events

### 6.3 Experiment Logging

Every experiment is logged with:

- Timestamp
- What was changed (diff from previous configuration)
- Which task(s) were affected
- Parent node in the solution tree (for Task B) — which deal or configuration this branched from
- Input evidence snapshot (or reference to evidence pack version)
- Raw outputs (forecasts, deal proposals)
- Scores on all metrics
- Reflective diagnosis (natural-language explanation of why this experiment succeeded or failed)
- Whether the change was retained or discarded
- Cost (tokens consumed, wall-clock time)

These logs are fully public and browsable on the site. For Task B, the solution tree is also visualizable — visitors can see the full exploration history including branches, dead ends, and backtrack points.

## 7. Public Website — Features and Pages

### 7.1 Landing Page

The latest AI-suggested peace proposal is the hero of the entire site. Visitors should land and immediately see what AutoPeace thinks the best feasible deal looks like right now.

- **Hero section:** The interactive world map (see Section 7.11) with the current best AI-proposed deal overlaid. Each stakeholder country glows green/amber/red based on simulated acceptance. Above the map: a one-sentence mission statement — "AI-powered automated research searching for solutions to the Iran conflict — fully open, fully transparent."
- **Deal headline card:** Directly below (or beside) the map, a bold, plain-language summary of the current AI-proposed deal — its key terms in 3-5 bullet points, its composite score, and how many autoresearch cycles have refined it. This is the single most important element on the page. It should answer the question: "What does AI think a workable deal looks like?"
- **Deal vs. real proposals:** A prominent comparison strip showing the AI-proposed deal side by side with real-world proposals currently on the table (see Section 7.4). At launch, this includes the US 15-point plan and Iran's 5-point counterproposal. Each shows a quick acceptance/rejection summary — making the gap between real proposals and the AI-generated alternative immediately visible.
- **Live headline metrics:** Floating or docked bar showing current top forecast (most likely outcome + probability), current best deal composite score, total experiments run, days since launch.
- **Cost-of-war ticker:** A running counter showing estimated global costs accumulating in real time — drawing visitors deeper into the cost-of-war explorer.
- **Progress spark:** A compact animated chart showing the system's deal composite score and Brier score improving over time — the visual proof that the autoresearch loop is working.
- **Call to action:** Explore the full deal, compare with real proposals, explore forecasts, view cost of war, view methodology, view source code.

### 7.2 Forecast Dashboard (Task A)

- **Current forecast:** Interactive visualization showing probability distribution across eight outcome categories, for each time horizon (30d, 90d, 180d, 1y).
- **Forecast history:** Timeline showing how the probability distribution has evolved over successive cycles. Users can scrub through time to see how the system's view has changed.
- **Calibration scorecard:** Honest display of how well-calibrated past forecasts have been. Calibration curve, Brier score trend, log score trend.
- **Prediction market comparison:** Side-by-side display of AutoPeace forecasts vs. Metaculus and Polymarket odds on comparable questions.
- **Evidence audit:** For each forecast, expandable view of which evidence items most influenced the prediction, with links to sources.
- **Rationale narrative:** Plain-language explanation of why the system currently forecasts what it forecasts.

### 7.3 Deal Dashboard (Task B)

- **Current best deal:** Structured term sheet displayed as an interactive card layout — one card per deal dimension (military, sanctions, diplomatic, etc.). This is the highest-composite-score deal from the Pareto frontier.
- **Pareto frontier view:** Display of alternative deals from the Pareto frontier, each labeled with the dimension it excels on (e.g., "Most Robust," "Most Acceptable to Iran," "Most Domestically Sellable in the US"). Visitors can compare tradeoffs and see what you gain and lose with each approach.
- **Solution tree explorer:** Visual tree showing the full exploration history for deal design — branches tried, dead ends, backtrack points. Each node is clickable to see the deal at that point and why the system branched or abandoned it.
- **Stakeholder acceptance map:** The interactive world map (see Section 7.11) with each stakeholder country glowing green (accept), amber (conditional), or red (reject). Click any country for detailed rationale. Also available as a sortable list view.
- **Domestic sellability diagnostic:** For each stakeholder's internal audiences, a breakdown of who supports and who opposes the deal, with modeled reasons.
- **Deal history:** Timeline of how the best proposed deal has evolved over successive cycles. Which terms changed and why.
- **Robustness report:** Results of red-team and spoiler stress tests. What attacks were tried, which ones the deal survived, which ones exposed vulnerabilities.
- **Deal comparison view:** Side-by-side comparison of the current best deal vs. earlier versions, or vs. real-world proposals (see Section 7.4), or vs. historical deals (e.g., JCPOA structure).

### 7.4 Real-World Proposal Testing (The Proposal Arena)

One of AutoPeace's most important features. The system does not only generate its own AI-proposed deals — it also ingests, structures, and scores real proposals from real players using the exact same multi-agent simulation pipeline. This creates a direct, apples-to-apples comparison between what real actors are proposing and what the AI system thinks is most feasible.

This is what makes AutoPeace genuinely useful rather than an academic exercise. A visitor can see: "The US proposed X. Iran proposed Y. Both were rejected. Here's what AutoPeace's AI thinks could actually bridge the gap — and here's how each proposal scores against the same criteria."

#### Launch Proposals

At launch, the Proposal Arena should include at least:

**1. The US 15-Point Plan (March 2026)**
Transmitted to Iran via Pakistan. Key reported terms include: dismantling of Iran's nuclear enrichment capabilities and sites, rollback of ballistic missile program, reopening of the Strait of Hormuz as a free maritime zone, cessation of support for proxy groups, enhanced IAEA monitoring. In exchange: lifting of nuclear-related sanctions, US assistance with civilian nuclear energy (Bushehr plant), removal of the snapback sanctions mechanism. The US also proposed a one-month ceasefire to negotiate details. Iran publicly rejected this as "maximalist and unreasonable."

**2. Iran's 5-Point Counterproposal (March 2026)**
Published via Press TV and Iranian embassies. Conditions: complete halt to US/Israeli aggression and assassinations, concrete mechanisms to prevent recurrence of war, guaranteed payment of war damages and reparations, end of hostilities on all fronts including against Hezbollah and Iraqi militias, and international recognition of Iran's sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz. The US has signaled these terms — particularly reparations and Hormuz sovereignty — are unacceptable.

**3. AutoPeace AI-Generated Proposal (Ongoing)**
The system's own best-scoring deal, continuously refined by the autoresearch loop. This is the system's attempt to find the feasible middle ground that real proposals have not yet reached.

#### How Real Proposals Are Processed

1. When a real-world proposal becomes public, it is manually or semi-automatically entered into the system using the same structured deal schema as AI-generated deals (military steps, sanctions steps, diplomatic steps, etc.).
2. The proposal is then run through the identical multi-agent evaluation pipeline: stakeholder agents evaluate it, domestic audience agents test sellability, red-team agents stress-test it, and judge agents score it.
3. The result is a full scorecard — the same seven scoring dimensions used for AI-generated deals — allowing direct comparison.

#### Proposal Arena UI

- **Comparison table:** All active proposals (real and AI-generated) displayed side by side, with scores on each dimension. The AI proposal is visually highlighted but not privileged — the scores speak for themselves.
- **Per-stakeholder breakdown:** For each proposal, click to see which stakeholders accept, reject, or conditionally accept — and why. This is where visitors see the structural gaps: "Iran rejects the US plan because of X; the US rejects Iran's plan because of Y; the AI proposal attempts to bridge this by Z."
- **Gap analysis:** An explicit visualization of where real proposals fail — which stakeholders reject them, which domestic audiences can't sell them, which red-team attacks break them. This highlights the specific sticking points that any viable deal must address.
- **Score evolution:** As new real proposals emerge (from any party), they are added to the arena and scored. Over time, visitors can see whether real-world proposals are converging toward or diverging from what the simulation predicts is feasible.
- **"What would it take?" feature:** For each real proposal, the system shows what minimal modifications would be needed to make it pass the scoring threshold — e.g., "If the US plan added reparations language and softened the missile demands, stakeholder acceptability rises from 0.3 to 0.6."

#### Adding New Real-World Proposals

- Any publicly reported proposal from any party (not just US and Iran — Gulf states, UN, European mediators, etc.) can be entered into the system.
- The admin panel includes a "Add Real Proposal" interface where the proposal is structured into the deal schema.
- In Phase 3, this could be opened to the public — visitors submit proposals they've seen in the news, and the system evaluates them.

### 7.5 Cost-of-War Explorer

- **Map-driven navigation:** The interactive world map (see Section 7.11) is the primary interface. Click any stakeholder country to see its specific costs. Toggle between economic, humanitarian, and strategic cost layers on the map.
- **Global overview:** Aggregate economic, humanitarian, and strategic costs of continued conflict, updated with latest available data, displayed as bold headline figures above the map.
- **Stakeholder drill-down:** Select any stakeholder (via map click or dropdown) and see a detailed cost breakdown with sourced figures and uncertainty ranges.
- **Deal-vs-war comparison:** Side-by-side view showing, for any stakeholder, the estimated costs of continued war vs. the estimated benefits of the current best deal. The map toggles between a "war costs" view (red) and a "deal benefits" view (green) to make the contrast visceral.
- **Live accumulator:** An animated counter showing costs accruing in real time — "Since you opened this page, the estimated cost of continued conflict has increased by $X." Designed to be emotionally striking and shareable.
- **Time dimension:** How costs accumulate over time — "every month of continued conflict costs X."
- **Sourcing and uncertainty:** Every figure links to its source. Uncertain estimates are flagged with ranges, not false precision.

### 7.6 Methodology and Transparency

- **How it works:** Plain-language explanation of the autoresearch loop, forecasting pipeline, and deal-design pipeline.
- **Stakeholder cards:** Full profiles for every modeled stakeholder, viewable by anyone. These are the "characters" in the simulation.
- **Domestic audience cards:** Sub-profiles for internal constituencies.
- **Scoring rules:** Complete documentation of how forecasts and deals are scored.
- **Limitations page:** A prominent, honest page explaining what LLM-based simulations can and cannot do, citing the relevant research on both capabilities and confounding risks.

### 7.7 Experiment Log

- **Searchable, filterable log** of every autoresearch experiment.
- **Each entry shows:** What was tried, what scored, whether it was kept.
- **Aggregate stats:** Total experiments, retention rate, score improvement trend.

### 7.8 Cycle Changelog ("What Changed")

A dedicated, plain-language feed designed for casual visitors who want to follow AutoPeace's progress without diving into raw experiment data. Think of it as release notes for each autoresearch cycle.

- **Chronological feed:** Each entry corresponds to one autoresearch cycle and is dated. Visitors can scroll through the full history or filter by date range.
- **Cycle summary card:** Each entry includes:
  - **Headline:** A one-sentence summary of the most important change, auto-generated or admin-edited. E.g., "Forecast shifted: major escalation probability rose from 12% to 19% after new troop deployment evidence." Or: "Deal updated: revised sanctions sequencing improved composite score by 8%."
  - **Forecast delta:** What the forecast looked like before vs. after this cycle, with the specific probabilities that moved and a plain-language explanation of why. Highlighted in green (improved calibration) or amber (lateral move).
  - **Deal delta:** If deal terms changed, a visual diff showing which terms were added, removed, or modified — displayed as simple before/after cards, not raw diffs. E.g., "Sanctions relief timeline changed from 6 months to 12 months (reason: domestic sellability in Iran improved by 15%)."
  - **Score movement:** Compact sparkline or arrow indicators showing how Brier score, deal composite score, and individual scoring dimensions moved.
  - **Key evidence:** The 1-3 evidence items that most influenced changes in this cycle, with links to sources.
  - **Experiments tried:** How many experiments were run, how many were retained, and a one-line summary of the most impactful retained change.
- **"Nothing changed" entries:** If a cycle ran but retained no changes, this is still logged with an explanation — e.g., "3 experiments run, none improved scores. Current configuration held." Transparency about non-progress is as important as progress.
- **Shareable entries:** Each changelog entry has a permalink and a share button that generates a social card summarizing the cycle's key change.
- **Subscribe:** Visitors can subscribe (via email or RSS) to receive changelog updates at the configured cadence.

### 7.9 Interactive Features (Light Interaction)

- **Time-horizon filter:** Toggle between 30d, 90d, 180d, 1y views for forecasts.

- **Stakeholder comparison:** Select two or more stakeholders and compare their costs, deal benefits, and acceptance profiles side by side.
- **Evidence explorer:** Browse the evidence corpus, filter by date/type/source, see which evidence items influenced which forecasts.

### 7.11 Visual Design and World Map

AutoPeace must be visually striking. The site is competing for public attention against social media, news sites, and entertainment — it cannot look like an academic paper or an internal dashboard. The goal is to make people stop, look, understand, and share.

#### Design Principles

- **Dark theme with high-contrast data.** A dark background (deep navy or near-black) makes data visualizations pop and conveys seriousness without being dreary. Accent colors should be warm (amber/gold for deal progress, red-orange for conflict/costs, green-teal for peace/benefits).
- **Cinematic, not cluttered.** Every page should have a clear visual focal point. White space is a feature, not a waste. Data density increases as users drill deeper — the surface layer is clean and bold.
- **Motion with purpose.** Subtle animations signal that the system is alive and working — a slowly rotating globe, gently pulsing data points, smooth transitions between views. Never decorative spinning or bouncing.
- **Mobile-first visual hierarchy.** The most important visual (the map, the headline forecast) must be immediately compelling on a phone screen.

#### Interactive World Map — Core Visualization

The world map is the centerpiece of the site and appears on the landing page, cost-of-war explorer, and deal dashboard. It should be implemented as an interactive WebGL globe (e.g., using Globe.gl, Cesium, Three.js globe, or Mapbox GL) or as a high-quality 2D map (e.g., D3.js with a custom projection) — the Replit agent should choose based on performance and complexity tradeoffs.

##### Map Layers (Toggleable)

| Layer | What It Shows | Visual Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Stakeholder countries** | All modeled stakeholders, color-coded by role (core principal, Gulf state, regional broker, external power) | Country fills with distinct hue per role category |
| **Conflict zones** | Active conflict areas, military incidents, strike locations | Pulsing red-orange markers or heat map overlay |
| **Shipping lanes** | Strait of Hormuz, Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb, Suez Canal | Animated flowing lines showing trade routes; color shifts from green to red based on disruption level |
| **Cost flows** | Economic impact flows between countries (sanctions, oil trade, military aid) | Animated arc lines with thickness proportional to dollar value |
| **Deal acceptance** | When viewing a deal, each stakeholder country glows green (accept), amber (conditional), or red (reject) | Country fill color with acceptance status |
| **Humanitarian impact** | Refugee flows, casualty density, infrastructure damage | Directional flow arrows for displacement; intensity overlay for damage |

##### Map Interactions

- **Click a country** → Sidebar or modal opens with that stakeholder's profile, costs, deal position, and domestic audience breakdown.
- **Hover over a shipping lane** → Tooltip shows current disruption level, insurance cost increase, and daily economic impact.
- **Toggle between "Cost of War" and "Benefits of Deal" views** → The map's color scheme shifts to show the contrast — red/dark for war costs, green/bright for deal benefits.
- **Time scrubber** → Slide through time to see how conflict events, costs, and forecasts have evolved. The map animates to show the progression.

##### Map on Different Pages

- **Landing page:** Globe view with all layers available, auto-rotating slowly, with key metrics floating alongside.
- **Cost-of-war explorer:** Map is the primary navigation — click countries to drill into their specific costs. Toggle between economic, humanitarian, and strategic cost layers.
- **Deal dashboard:** Map shows the acceptance map — which countries accept the current best deal, which reject, which are conditional. Click to see rationale.
- **Forecast dashboard:** Map shows recent evidence events geographically — where military incidents, diplomatic meetings, and economic shocks are happening.

#### Other Key Visualizations

- **Forecast probability fan chart:** A fan-shaped area chart showing how probability distributions across the eight outcomes have evolved over time. Wide fan = high uncertainty. Narrowing fan = increasing confidence. This should be visually beautiful — smooth gradients, not blocky stacked bars.
- **Deal evolution timeline:** A horizontal timeline showing how the best deal's terms have changed across autoresearch cycles. Each term category (military, sanctions, diplomatic, etc.) gets a swim lane. Changes are highlighted with visual diffs.
- **Stakeholder acceptance radar:** A radar/spider chart for each deal showing scores across the seven scoring dimensions. Overlaying multiple deals on the same radar makes comparison intuitive.
- **Cost-of-war accumulator:** An animated counter or rising bar showing how costs accumulate in real time — "Since you opened this page, the estimated cost of continued conflict has increased by $X." This is the kind of detail that gets shared on social media.
- **Calibration bullseye:** A custom visualization for the calibration scorecard — concentric rings representing calibration buckets, with dots showing where forecasts landed. Tight clustering near the center = good calibration.
- **Experiment improvement curve:** A clean line chart showing composite scores improving over autoresearch cycles, with annotations for major methodology changes.

#### Shareability

Every major visualization should have a "Share" button that generates a static image or Open Graph preview suitable for sharing on X, LinkedIn, and other platforms. The shared image should include the AutoPeace branding, the key data point, and a URL back to the live site. This is critical for organic reach.

## 8. Data Pipeline

### 8.1 Evidence Sources

The evidence ingestion pipeline draws from multiple tiers, ranging from real-time social and news feeds to slower-moving institutional data. Sources are organized by update frequency.

#### Real-Time Sources (Minutes to Hours)

| Source | Type | Update Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| X.com (Twitter) API | Official government/leader accounts, journalist posts, OSINT analysts, public sentiment signals | Real-time / polling every 15-60 minutes |
| Google News API | Aggregated breaking news from multiple outlets | Real-time / polling every 30 minutes |
| NewsAPI.org | Structured news articles from 150k+ sources, filterable by topic and region | Real-time / polling every 30 minutes |
| Reuters / AP live feeds | Wire service breaking news via RSS/Atom or API | Continuous / polling every 15 minutes |
| Telegram channel monitoring | OSINT channels, regional conflict-reporting channels, official government channels (particularly Iranian, Iraqi, Yemeni sources) | Polling every 30-60 minutes |

#### Daily Sources

| Source | Type | Update Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| GDELT | Global event data, news mentions, tone analysis | Daily |
| Official government statements | US, Iran, Israel, EU, UN press releases and transcripts | Daily |
| Prediction markets (Metaculus, Polymarket) | Market-implied probabilities for relevant questions | Daily |
| Oil and commodity markets | Brent crude, shipping indices, sovereign CDS spreads, freight rates | Daily |

#### Weekly to Monthly Sources

| Source | Type | Update Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| ACLED | Armed conflict events, fatalities, actors | Weekly |
| UN OCHA / UNHCR | Humanitarian situation reports, displacement data | Weekly |
| SIPRI | Military expenditure, arms transfers | Monthly / quarterly |
| IAEA reports | Nuclear program monitoring updates | As published |
| Think tank publications | IISS, CSIS, Brookings, Crisis Group, ICG analyses | As published |

#### Credibility Tiering

Not all evidence is equal. The pipeline assigns each evidence item a credibility tier that affects how it is weighted in the forecasting and deal-design pipelines:

| Tier | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1 — Official primary** | Direct statements, documents, or data from official sources | Government press releases, UN resolutions, IAEA reports, signed agreements, official military communiqués |
| **Tier 2 — Institutional analysis** | Verified reporting and analysis from established institutions | Major wire services (Reuters, AP, AFP), peer-reviewed research, SIPRI/ACLED data, prediction market prices |
| **Tier 3 — Expert and journalist** | Reporting and analysis from credentialed individuals | Named journalist reporting, verified analyst accounts on X, established think tank commentary |
| **Tier 4 — Open-source signal** | Unverified but potentially informative signals | Anonymous OSINT accounts, Telegram channels, social media sentiment, unconfirmed reports |

Tier 4 evidence is ingested for signal detection but is flagged as unverified and down-weighted in scoring. The system never bases a high-confidence forecast shift on Tier 4 evidence alone.

#### X.com API — Specific Design Considerations

The X.com API is a high-value but noisy source. The pipeline should:

- Maintain curated lists of tracked accounts organized by stakeholder and role (heads of state, foreign ministers, defense officials, UN officials, major journalists covering the conflict, established OSINT analysts).
- Run sentiment and topic classification on ingested posts to filter conflict-relevant content.
- Track engagement velocity on key posts as a proxy for signal importance.
- Flag potential misinformation (unverified claims, posts from low-credibility accounts, viral content without sourcing) and route it to Tier 4.
- Respect API rate limits and cost. The admin panel should include X API usage monitoring and the ability to adjust polling frequency or disable the source entirely.

#### Cost Awareness

Real-time sources consume significantly more API quota and processing than weekly/monthly sources. The admin panel should expose per-source cost tracking and allow the admin to enable/disable individual sources or adjust polling frequencies without code changes.

### 8.2 Evidence Processing

- Each evidence item is timestamped, source-tagged, credibility-tiered, and stored with metadata.
- Evidence is chunked, embedded, and indexed for retrieval by the forecasting and deal-design agents.
- Evidence packs are versioned — each autoresearch cycle records exactly which evidence was available to the system at runtime.
- A deduplication and quality filter removes duplicate reports, retractions, and low-signal noise.
- For real-time sources, a batching layer aggregates items between autoresearch cycles so that each cycle receives a coherent evidence snapshot rather than a raw stream.
- High-signal breaking events (e.g., a military strike, an official negotiation announcement) should be flagged for potential immediate-trigger runs if the admin has enabled event-triggered cycles.

### 8.3 Evidence Integrity

- The system never uses evidence from after a forecast cutoff date during backtesting.
- Evidence provenance is tracked and auditable.
- When evidence items are contradictory, the system flags this rather than silently resolving the contradiction.
- Credibility tier is recorded as immutable metadata — the system does not retroactively upgrade unverified evidence.

## 9. Credibility and Trust Features

### 9.1 Calibration Scorecard

A permanently visible element on the forecast dashboard showing:

- Historical calibration curve (predicted probability vs. actual frequency)
- Rolling Brier score trend
- Rolling log score trend
- Count of forecasts made and evaluated
- Explicit display of the system's biggest misses, not just its hits

### 9.2 Prediction Market Benchmark

Side-by-side comparison of AutoPeace forecasts vs. Metaculus and Polymarket odds on comparable questions. This gives visitors an independent reference point. If AutoPeace consistently underperforms prediction markets, that's shown honestly.

### 9.3 Limitations Disclosure

A dedicated, prominent page (linked from every dashboard) covering:

- LLM simulations are instruments for structured experimentation, not substitutes for real humans
- Simulated stakeholder acceptance does not equal real-world acceptance
- The system is vulnerable to prompt-design artifacts and confounding (citing the 2025 causal-inference research)
- Forecasts are probabilistic and will sometimes be wrong
- The system has no access to classified intelligence, private diplomatic channels, or real decision-makers' actual thinking
- Cost-of-war estimates rely on published data and may be incomplete or contested

### 9.4 Versioning and Audit Trail

- Every forecast, deal proposal, score, and methodology change is versioned with a timestamp.
- The full history is browsable and downloadable.
- Nothing is silently edited or deleted.

## 10. Technical Architecture

### 10.1 Deployment Target

The application will be built and deployed on **Replit**. The tech stack should be chosen by Replit's AI agent for optimal compatibility, with the following constraints and recommendations:

#### Constraints

- Must support scheduled background jobs (the autoresearch loop) — use Replit's built-in scheduled tasks, or a cron-like mechanism within the app.
- Must support a persistent database for storing forecasts, deals, experiment logs, evidence metadata, and scores.
- Must support server-side API calls to multiple LLM providers (Anthropic Claude, OpenAI GPT, and Google Gemini). Different agents in the pipeline use different providers for cross-model evaluation integrity.
- Must serve a responsive web frontend accessible on desktop and mobile.

#### Recommendations for the Replit Agent

- A full-stack JavaScript/TypeScript framework (e.g., Next.js) is likely the simplest single-repo approach on Replit.
- For the database, SQLite (via better-sqlite3 or Drizzle) is simplest for a solo developer on Replit; PostgreSQL (via Replit's managed Postgres) is better if data volume grows.
- For the LLM orchestration layer, use the Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google Gemini SDKs. The system has API access to all three providers, and different agents in the pipeline should use models from different providers (e.g., Claude for deal generation, GPT for stakeholder evaluation, Gemini for red-teaming — the exact assignment can itself be varied by the autoresearch loop). Each agent is a function call with its own system prompt, context, and model assignment. The pipeline should be modular so that swapping a model for any agent is a configuration change, not a code change.
- For evidence ingestion, use scheduled fetch jobs that pull from APIs (GDELT, ACLED, prediction markets) and RSS/Atom feeds (wire services, think tanks).
- For the vector store (evidence retrieval), a lightweight solution like chromadb or a simple embedding index is sufficient at MVP scale.
- For the interactive world map, consider Globe.gl (WebGL globe, visually stunning, lightweight), react-globe.gl (React wrapper), Mapbox GL JS (2D/3D map with excellent interactivity), or D3.js with topojson (lighter weight, full control). Choose based on performance on Replit's hosting and visual impact. The map is the most important visual element on the site — prioritize visual quality and smooth interaction.
- For charts and data visualization, use Recharts (React-native), D3.js, or Plotly for the forecast probability charts, calibration displays, and experiment improvement curves.

### 10.3 Admin Panel

A password-protected admin interface with:

- **Cadence control:** Set autoresearch run frequency (hourly / daily / every N days / weekly / manual).
- **Budget control:** Set maximum token spend per cycle, with alerts when approaching limits.
- **Pause / resume:** Halt or restart the autoresearch loop.
- **Force-run:** Trigger an immediate cycle (e.g., after a major news event).
- **Evidence source management:** Enable/disable individual evidence sources, add new sources.
- **Experiment queue:** View pending, running, and completed experiments.
- **Cost monitoring:** Track cumulative API costs per provider (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google), per-cycle costs, and cost trends.
- **Model assignment:** Configure which LLM provider and model is used for each agent role (proposal, stakeholder, domestic audience, red-team, judge, meta-evaluator). Defaults to cross-provider assignment for evaluation integrity.
- **Real-world proposal management:** Interface to add, edit, and re-score real-world proposals. Includes a structured form matching the deal schema, fields for source URLs and known real-world responses, and a button to trigger multi-agent evaluation against the current evidence pack.

## 15. Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Severity | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| LLM simulation does not meaningfully predict real outcomes | High | Prominent limitations disclosure; benchmark against prediction markets; focus on calibration improvement, not accuracy claims |
| API costs exceed budget | Medium | Configurable cadence; budget caps; token monitoring; start with smaller evidence packs and scale up |
| System produces a deal that is politically inflammatory or insensitive | Medium | Neutrality guidelines in all agent prompts; no political advocacy; frame all outputs as "the simulation produced X," not "the solution is X" |
| Public misinterprets simulation results as real predictions | High | Mandatory disclaimers on every page; limitations page linked everywhere; calibration scorecard shows honest performance |
| Data sources become unavailable or change format | Medium | Modular ingestion pipeline; graceful degradation if a source fails; multiple sources per evidence type |
| Reward hacking in the autoresearch loop | Low | Cross-model evaluation (different LLM providers for generation vs. evaluation); public audit trail; simplicity criterion |
| Single developer bottleneck | Medium | Open-source from day one; clear contributing guide; modular architecture |

## 17. Appendix: Relevant Research

### Autoresearch Ecosystem

- **Karpathy's Autoresearch (March 2026)** — the methodological inspiration. Key design principles adopted by AutoPeace: fixed evaluation metric, keep/discard loop, `program.md` as the human steering lever, simplicity criterion. 42k+ GitHub stars.
- **AIDE / Weco AI** — tree-search-based ML engineering agent. Key idea adopted: solution tree structure for exploring fundamentally different approaches in parallel rather than linear hill-climbing. State of the art on OpenAI's MLE-Bench (75 Kaggle competitions). AutoPeace uses this pattern for deal design exploration.
- **GEPA (Genetic-Pareto, ICLR 2026 Oral)** — reflective prompt evolution using natural-language diagnosis and Pareto-based selection. Key ideas adopted: (1) maintain a Pareto frontier of candidate deals rather than a single best, (2) use reflective diagnosis ("why did this fail?") rather than just scalar scores to guide the next experiment, (3) Pareto selection to maintain diverse exploration. Outperforms RL (GRPO) by 6% average using 35x fewer rollouts.
- **SkyPilot parallel autoresearch** — demonstrated that parallel exploration catches interaction effects that sequential search misses. 910 experiments in 8 hours on 16 GPUs. AutoPeace should explore deal variants in parallel when budget allows.
- **Autoresearch reward hacking** — documented by community users (tennis match prediction). AutoPeace mitigates primarily through cross-model evaluation (using different LLM providers for generation vs. evaluation).
